A set of icons that tells anyone, at a glance, what a service or event will actually be like — how loud, how busy, how long, and whether they will be asked to take part. Built from the predictability model: when the conditions are known in advance, they hold a person up rather than hold them down.

The What to Expect strip

Six measures and the quiet space, one row. It goes on the flyer, the email, the website page, the order of service and the noticeboard — always the same measures, always in the same order, so people learn to read it once and then read it everywhere.

  • One picture per measureThe speaker always means volume, the stopwatch always means length. The picture never changes with the level, so it can be learned once.
  • The bars carry the levelOne bar, two bars, three bars, beside every label. A tick or a cross for quiet space. Anyone can read them without reading English, and they survive a black-and-white photocopy.
  • No red, amber, greenA traffic light says loud is bad. Loud is not bad — it is just loud, and some people are coming precisely for that.
  • Say it, then honour itThe strip is a promise. If it says an hour and a half, the service ends at an hour and a half.

Why this is not a food label

Traffic-light nutrition labelling works because there is a right answer: less salt is better. There is no right answer here. A quiet, seated, 40-minute service with a printed order is not a better service than a loud, full, 100-minute one. It is a different one, and different people need each. So the system reports conditions — it never grades them.

This matters practically as well as ethically. The moment a red badge appears on the 10.30 service, the 10.30 team will start arguing with the strip instead of filling it in honestly. Neutral reporting is what keeps it accurate, and accuracy is the entire value of the thing.

The QR code

Every graphic from the maker can carry a small QR code in the bottom corner. It opens What the symbols mean, a page for visitors that explains every picture, every level and the bars, and starts with the levels for that exact event, in full sentences.

  • Leave the link as it isThe maker adds the event’s levels to the end of the link (?s=2222210001), so the page can show them. Paste a different link only if the event has its own page.
  • Keep it at least 2 cm wide in printSmaller than that and older phones struggle to read it. On screen it can be smaller.
  • A new strip means a new codeIf you change a level, download the graphic again. The old code still shows the old levels.

  • Minimum size24 px on screen, 8 mm in print. Below that the bars stop separating from each other.
  • Clear spaceHalf an icon width on all sides. Icons never touch each other or a rule.
  • Do not recolour by levelEverything in a strip is the same colour. Level is carried by the bars and the word, never by colour.
  • Do not add new levelsThree steps only. A five-point scale is harder to fill in honestly and harder to read quickly.

How to set a level honestly

  • Set it for a normal week, not a good oneIf more than 50 come one Sunday in four, it is 50-150, not 0-50.
  • When you are between two levels, choose the higherSomeone arriving to find it quieter than promised is fine. The reverse is not.
  • Review it termlyServices drift. Put it on the same cycle as the rota build.
  • One person owns each event’s stripThe ministry lead sets it; the comms lead checks it is on the material. Tier 0 — no sign-off needed.
  • Change it when the event changesMoving to the school hall changes seating, personal space and quiet space. Update before you publish.
  • Never leave a measure blankA missing badge reads as a hidden answer. If you do not know yet, say “we will confirm this by Friday”.
